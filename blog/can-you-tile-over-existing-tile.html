Can You Tile Over Existing Tile? An Honest Answer

Sometimes it's a perfectly sound way to save a demo day. Sometimes it's how you end up paying for the same floor twice. Here's how to tell which situation you're in.

Tile Mississippi•Floors & Prep

The short answer: often yes — but only if the tile underneath is genuinely sound, and only if you've accounted for the height you're adding. Tiling over existing tile is a legitimate technique, not a shortcut a good installer refuses on principle. What it is not is a way to make a failing tile job go away. New tile bonded to old tile that's coming loose will come loose too, just with a more expensive surface on top.

This question comes up constantly, usually because tear-out is the dusty, disruptive, expensive-feeling part of a tile job. Skipping it is genuinely appealing. So let's go through when it works.

When Tiling Over Existing Tile Works

Going over the old tile is reasonable when all of the following are true:

  • Every tile is solidly bonded. We tap across the whole floor listening for hollow spots. A hollow sound means the tile has lost its bond to the substrate. Even a few loose tiles means the new floor has soft spots built into it from day one.
  • The surface is flat. Old tile that's lippy, dished, or wavy transfers all of that into the new layer, and modern large-format tile is far less forgiving of an uneven base than the small tile it's often replacing.
  • There's no moisture problem. Any staining, efflorescence, musty smell, or history of leaks means finding the source first. Sealing a moisture problem under another layer of tile guarantees a bigger repair later.
  • No cracks that indicate movement. A crack running through multiple tiles in a line usually means the slab or subfloor below is moving. That movement will telegraph straight through new tile unless it's properly addressed.
  • The added height doesn't cause problems. More on this below — it's the one most people miss.
  • The substrate can carry the weight. On a wood-framed floor, you're adding a second full layer of tile and mortar. On a concrete slab this is a non-issue; on an older framed floor it's worth a look.

When the Old Tile Has to Come Out

Tear it out when:

  • Tiles are loose, hollow-sounding, cracked, or already lifting
  • There's any sign of water damage or a soft subfloor
  • The floor flexes or feels springy underfoot — that's a structural issue, and tile won't fix it
  • You're tiling a shower or a wet area (see below)
  • The height gain would break a clearance you can't work around
  • The existing tile was installed over something questionable to begin with, like vinyl or a floating floor

None of this is about being purist. It's that the labor to set new tile is the same either way — so bonding good tile to a bad base spends the full installation cost on a floor with a known expiration date.

The Height Problem Nobody Thinks About

This is the one that catches homeowners off guard. Going over existing tile means the new floor sits higher by the thickness of the new tile, plus the mortar bed, plus any membrane. That stack adds up fast, and a floor doesn't exist in isolation:

  • Doors. Interior doors and especially exterior doors may no longer clear the floor. Doors can be trimmed, but an exterior door with a threshold is a bigger problem.
  • Appliances. A dishwasher lives in a fixed-height opening under the countertop. Raise the kitchen floor and it may no longer come out for service — a genuinely common and genuinely miserable discovery.
  • Transitions. Where the new tile meets carpet, wood, or vinyl in the next room, the height difference has to be resolved with a transition strip. A big step reads as an afterthought and is a trip hazard.
  • Toilet flange. In a bathroom, raising the floor around the toilet means the flange sits low relative to the finished floor. That has to be corrected properly with an extender, or you risk a bad wax seal — which is a leak at the base of the toilet.
  • Cabinet toe kicks and stair risers. Both get visibly shorter. On stairs, an uneven riser height at the top or bottom of a flight is a real safety issue.

All of these are solvable when they're identified during the quote. They're expensive when they're discovered with mortar already on the floor.

Showers Are a Special Case — Don't

We'll say this plainly: tiling over existing tile inside a shower is a bad idea in almost every case. The waterproofing that matters lives behind the original tile, and going over the top means you can't inspect it, can't test it, and can't correct it. You're also adding weight to a wall assembly and making the niche, mixing valve, and drain details nearly impossible to seal properly at the new tile plane.

If a shower needs new tile, it needs to be opened up. That's also the only way to find out whether there's damage behind it — which, in our experience in this climate, there often is. Our post on shower waterproofing mistakes covers what should be back there.

The Prep That Makes It Work

When we do tile over existing tile, the prep is most of the job:

  1. Sound the whole surface. Tap everything, mark hollow spots, and remove and patch anything that isn't bonded.
  2. Clean it properly. Years of mopping leave soap film, wax, and residue. Mortar won't bond to any of it. Kitchens get degreased.
  3. Abrade the glaze. Glazed tile is smooth and non-porous — exactly what mortar doesn't like. The surface gets mechanically roughened or treated with a bonding primer made for the purpose.
  4. Flatten it. Low spots get filled with a patching compound so the new layer starts flat.
  5. Add an uncoupling or anti-fracture membrane. On most floors we'd specify one. It lets the old layer and the new layer move independently, so a crack or a seasonal shift underneath doesn't telegraph through to the tile you just paid for.
  6. Use the right mortar. Bonding to a non-porous surface calls for a mortar rated for it, not a basic thin-set.

Skip these and the new tile is a coin flip. Do them and tile-over-tile can last as long as a standard installation.

Tile Over Tile vs. Tear-Out: How to Decide

Tiling over saves the demo labor, the dumpster, and a day or two of disruption, and it avoids the risk of damaging a slab during removal. Tear-out costs more up front but keeps your floor heights where they are, lets you see and fix what's underneath, and gives the new tile the best possible base.

Our rule of thumb: if the existing tile is solid and the height works, going over it is a sound way to spend less. If there's any question about what's under there — especially moisture — tear it out. The demo is the cheap part of the job. Redoing the whole floor in three years is not.

Common Questions

Can you tile over existing tile?

Often yes, on floors and walls, if the existing tile is completely solid, well bonded, flat, and free of moisture problems, and if the added height does not cause clearance issues. It is not a shortcut around a failing installation — if any part of the old tile is loose, hollow, cracked from movement, or sitting on a damaged substrate, it has to come out.

How much height does tiling over tile add?

You add the thickness of the new tile plus the mortar bed, and often an uncoupling membrane on top of that. On a floor, that stack is usually enough to affect door clearance, appliance height under a counter, transitions to adjoining rooms, and the toilet flange in a bathroom. Those clearances should be checked before the job starts, not discovered mid-install.

Can you tile over tile in a shower?

We do not recommend it. Tiling over shower tile buries whatever waterproofing is behind the old tile without any way to inspect or test it, adds weight to the wall assembly, and makes the niche, valve, and drain details difficult to seal correctly. A shower is the one place where the right answer is almost always to tear out and rebuild.

How do you prepare old tile for new tile?

The old tile is sounded for hollow or loose spots, cleaned thoroughly to remove soap film, wax, and grease, and mechanically abraded or deglossed so mortar can bond to the glazed surface. Low areas get flattened with a patching compound, and many installations get an uncoupling or anti-fracture membrane so movement in the old tile does not telegraph into the new.
